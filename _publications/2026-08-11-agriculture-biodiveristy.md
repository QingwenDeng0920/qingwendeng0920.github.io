Agricultural modernization can raise yields while reducing the crop genetic resources maintained in farmers' fields. This paper develops a dynamic ecological-economic model of when that narrowing becomes a persistent loss of in situ diversity and when modernization can instead coexist with conservation.

The model couples three state variables: diversified local cultivation, genetic diversity within the landrace population, and institutional capacity for conservation and diversified extension. A fold structure in cultivation interacts with an extinction-vortex feedback between population size and genetic diversity, while institutional capacity acts as a slow-moving stock that dampens displacement pressure and raises the economic value of continued landrace cultivation. The resulting system contains a bistable region in which the same modernization pressure can produce sharply different long-run outcomes depending on the timing of institution-building.

Numerical experiments identify a sharp transition between trajectories that remain on a high-diversity branch and those that enter a functional low-diversity basin. An optimal-control benchmark shows that a forward-looking conservation planner front-loads institutional investment before varietal promotion intensifies and thereby avoids collapse. A delayed-recognition extension further shows how late policy attention can make recovery and abandonment nearly welfare-equivalent even while their biological outcomes remain sharply separated.

Paired illustrations from India's Green Revolution and Peru's Potato Park interpret the mechanism as a difference in sequencing: modernization that outpaces conservation capacity versus modernization embedded in institutions that keep crop diversity economically and socially active. The central implication is that genetic erosion is not an automatic cost of modernization; it is a risk created by promoting modernization before building the institutions needed to preserve, regenerate, and re-embed diversity.
