Political institutions do not merely govern pre-existing social groups; by allocating benefits through legally defined categories, they can reshape the identities citizens publicly claim. This paper studies this process using China's regional ethnic autonomy system, which links administrative classification to preferential policies in education, political representation, employment, and local development.

I develop a model of identity choice in which families weigh private preferences, reputational payoffs, and policy incentives. I test its predictions using China's 1982, 1990, 2000, and 2005 census and survey microdata with cohort difference-in-differences, event-study, placebo, and staggered-adoption-robust estimators. Prefecture-level autonomy establishment reduces the Han-identification rate among newborns of inter-ethnic families by 12.4 percentage points, while a county-level extension across multiple census waves shows effects that grow over time.

The analysis also reveals an important distinction in the politics of administrative reclassification. Centrally mandated designations from the 1950s pass placebo tests, whereas post-1978 designations associated with local petitioning exhibit substantial pre-treatment trends. The results show both how distributive institutions can reshape identity choices and when administrative reclassification can provide credible identifying variation.
