How do states allocate natural resources, and why do some allocations generate extraction rather than development? We study Cambodia's economic land concessions (ELCs), which transformed state-controlled land into privately enforceable commercial rights.

Combining spatial and cohort-based difference-in-differences designs with international rubber-price shocks interacted with predetermined suitability, we examine how resource rents are converted into property-right allocation and local outcomes. We find that ELCs increased forest loss both within concession boundaries and across receiving districts, while generating no comparable local development dividend: nighttime activity did not increase at project sites and declined in pre-existing settlements.

International rent exposure increased new concession grants after an administrative delay, and the same exposure produced greater extraction and weaker local development. Historical legacies further conditioned where extraction intensified. The findings show that property-right formalization can facilitate resource extraction without broad-based development when states allocate enforceable rights to actors with limited local economic linkages.
